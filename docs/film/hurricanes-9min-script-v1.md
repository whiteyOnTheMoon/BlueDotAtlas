## Sprechtext (Englisch)

### 1 · The storm that exploded (≈ 0:45)

In September 2026, a storm called Polo was moving over the eastern Pacific.

For a while, it looked ordinary.

Then, within a single day, its strongest winds rose by ninety knots. By the twenty-second of September, Polo had become a Category Five hurricane — the highest category on the scale.

Storms do not do this by chance. Polo had found everything it needed: warm water, moist air, and calm winds high above.

To understand how a storm can grow this fast, we have to look beneath it. At the ocean.

Because the ocean powers the storm.

### 2 · The engine (≈ 1:45)

A hurricane is a heat engine. Its fuel is warm, moist air from the tropical sea.

The sun heats the ocean, and water evaporates into the air above it. As a weather disturbance drifts across the tropics, that warm air rises into it — and leaves an area of low pressure underneath.

More air rushes in to take its place.

As the rising air climbs, it cools. Its water vapour condenses into droplets, building the towering clouds we see from space. And condensation releases heat — heat that first came from the ocean. That heat drives the air upward even faster, and draws in still more moist air from the sea.

The rotation of the Earth turns this inflow into a spin. When the winds reach seventy-four miles per hour, the storm is called a hurricane.

The scale of this engine is hard to imagine. According to NOAA, the winds of a single hurricane can release about half as much energy as all the world's power plants can generate. The forming of its clouds and rain releases hundreds of times more.

And all of it begins with warm water.

### 3 · The ocean's fuel tank (≈ 1:30)

How warm does the water need to be? Hurricanes form where the sea surface reaches at least about twenty-seven degrees Celsius — eighty degrees Fahrenheit. That is why they are born in the tropics.

But the surface is only part of the story.

As a hurricane moves, its winds churn the ocean and pull up cooler water from below. That cooling can starve the storm of its fuel.

So scientists also measure how deep the warm water reaches. NOAA calls this the tropical cyclone heat potential: the heat stored in the upper ocean, down to where the water cools below twenty-six degrees.

Where warm water runs deep — in warm ocean eddies, for example — a storm can keep drawing energy without cooling its own supply. If the winds above allow it, these are the places where a storm can strengthen suddenly.

In 2026, the eastern Pacific offered plenty of warm water.

### 4 · A record year (≈ 1:30)

That year, a strong El Niño took hold.

During El Niño, the surface of the central and eastern tropical Pacific grows warmer than normal. On this map from NASA, red shows water warmer than usual — in 2026, above, compared with the year before.

The result, according to NOAA's review of the season, was the most active hurricane season on record in the central and eastern Pacific.

Scientists measure a season's total strength as accumulated cyclone energy — how strong the storms were, and how long they lasted. In 2026, it reached three hundred fifty-nine. The previous record, from 2018, was three hundred eighteen.

In the Atlantic, the same El Niño helped to calm the season.

It is worth being precise here: NOAA links this record to El Niño — a natural pattern of the climate — not directly to global warming.

### 5 · Into the storm (≈ 1:30)

Forecasters watch every one of these storms. Satellites follow them day and night.

But some measurements can only be taken from inside. So NOAA sends its Hurricane Hunters: aircraft built to fly right into the storm. Two WP-3D Orion planes fly through the storm itself, while a jet cruises forty-five thousand feet above and around it.

They release instruments that fall through the storm, collecting data from its core and its surroundings. That data goes straight into NOAA's computer models — where it has helped improve hurricane track forecasts by about twenty percent.

Where a storm will go, forecasters can predict better than ever.

How strong it will become is harder. Rapid intensification — like Polo's — remains one of the toughest problems in forecasting. NOAA calls it the largest source of error in intensity forecasts.

### 6 · What comes next (≈ 1:15)

What happens as the planet keeps warming? Here, scientists speak carefully — and so should we.

Climate models at NOAA's Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory project that hurricanes will, on average, become more intense: by roughly one to ten percent, for two degrees of global warming.

A larger share of storms is projected to reach Category Four and Five.

Rainfall near the storm is projected to rise by about fourteen percent, because warmer air holds more moisture.

The total number of storms may not rise at all. Many models project fewer, or about the same.

And then there is the sea itself. As sea level rises, coastal flooding from hurricanes will increase — all else being equal.

### 7 · What to take with you (≈ 0:45)

So what can we learn from Polo?

A hurricane is not a random disaster. It is the ocean's heat, turned into wind and rain.

Where the water is warm, and the air allows it, a storm can grow faster than anyone expects. That is why it matters to watch the ocean closely — and to prepare before the storm arrives.

The ocean powers the storm.

And the ocean is changing.
